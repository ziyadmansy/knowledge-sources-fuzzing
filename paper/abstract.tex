An LLM-guided refinement loop for differential testing of Dart JSON
deserializers found divergence in 2.9\% of generated documents on its own,
but in 28.8\% when a human gave it four observations from a manual
characterization: the bottleneck was domain knowledge, and it came from a
person. We ask whether the fuzzer can acquire that knowledge itself. Holding
the loop fixed and varying only a 1{,}500-character knowledge slot, we
compare four sources in a pre-registered study: none, the human's
observations, a summary written by an LLM agent that reads the
deserializers' code, and a summary written by an agent that designs and runs
20 black-box probe documents. On Dart ($n{=}5$ seeds per arm), human
knowledge reaches 30.9\%, code-read knowledge 11.6\%, and no knowledge
1.5\% (each pair $p \approx 0.008$, Holm-adjusted $p \approx 0.048$).
Probing reaches 10.5\% on average, but not detectably above no knowledge
($p \approx 0.69$): it is all-or-nothing, 49\% on the one seed whose probes
happened to hit a divergence and at most 1.3\% on the other four. Which
known divergence patterns each arm finds tracks exactly which facts its
summary states. On a second ecosystem with no human characterization
(Kotlin: Gson, Moshi, kotlinx.serialization, Jackson), where divergence is
common, probing does help: 67.1\% against 50.0\% over ten seeds
($p \approx 0.007$, with a replication declared before it ran). Black-box
self-characterization works when the behaviours worth knowing are easy to
stumble on and fails when they are narrow, which is where knowledge matters
most. Triage of the Kotlin divergences found no new bug in the libraries'
documented defaults, and one standards violation in
kotlinx.serialization's strict mode that had been reported only
generically before.
